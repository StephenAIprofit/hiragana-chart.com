Dakuten, Handakuten and Yoon: The Complete Guide

The basic 46 hiragana take you a long way, but real Japanese needs more sounds, and those sounds do not come from new kana. They come from two small marks and one small kana: dakuten and handakuten change the sound of a kana, and yoon joins two kana into a single beat. Together they take the chart from 46 sounds to 107.

The chart shows all 107 sounds, and the quiz tests them in levels.

What dakuten does

Dakuten is the two short strokes ゛ at the top right of a kana. It turns a plain sound into a voiced sound, on four rows:

PlainVoicedRule
か き く け こが ぎ ぐ げ ごk becomes g
さ し す せ そざ じ ず ぜ ぞs becomes z
た ち つ て とだ ぢ づ で どt becomes d
は ひ ふ へ ほば び ぶ べ ぼh becomes b

That is 20 new sounds. The shape and stroke order stay the same: write か, then add the two strokes at the end.

Dakuten also shows up inside compound words: 人 is ひと (person), and two people is 人々, read ひとびと, where the second ひと becomes びと.

What handakuten does

Handakuten is the small circle ゜ at the top right of a kana. It works on one row only:

PlainWith handakutenRule
は ひ ふ へ ほぱ ぴ ぷ ぺ ぽh becomes p

That is 5 more sounds. Dakuten and handakuten together add 25, taking the chart from 46 to 71.

P sounds are rarer than voiced sounds. They mostly appear in doubled sounds and in words that mimic sounds or feelings: いっぱい means a lot; ほっぺ means cheek; ぴかぴか means sparkling. They are part of the 71, and the quiz tests them at L2.

When a mark changes the meaning

A dakuten mark is never decoration. Add it or drop it and the word changes. かき is an oyster, or a persimmon. かぎ is a key. せん is one thousand. ぜん is all, or entire. はな is a flower, or a nose. はなび is fireworks.

Guessing is risky: read かぎ as かき and you turn a key into an oyster. Check the top right of each kana before you decide what it says, and add the mark last when you write.

What yoon is

Yoon, or contracted sounds, is a small や, ゆ or よ after a kana from the i row. It joins with the kana before it to make a single beat:

Base+ ゃ+ ゅ+ ょ
ききゃ kyaきゅ kyuきょ kyo
ししゃ shaしゅ shuしょ sho
ちちゃ chaちゅ chuちょ cho

The same pattern works for に, ひ, み and り. The voiced rows work the same way: ぎゃ, じゃ, びゃ and ぴゃ, each with ゃ, ゅ and ょ. There is one more row, ぢゃ, ぢゅ and ぢょ, which sounds the same as じゃ, じゅ and じょ and is rarely used. The chart counts it: seven plain rows (21) plus five voiced rows (15) make 36, taking the chart from 71 to 107.

The key point is the beat: a small ゃ has no sound of its own. きゃ is one beat, きや is two.

Yoon appears in everyday words: きょう means today, しゃしん means a photo, ひゃく means one hundred, りょこう means travel, びょういん means hospital.

Same sound, different kana

Two pairs of kana sound exactly the same in modern Japanese:

  • じ and ぢ both sound like ji.
  • ず and づ both sound like zu.

The difference is spelling, not sound. Use じ and ず by default; ぢ and づ mostly appear inside compound words: 鼻血 is はなぢ (nosebleed), and 続く is つづく (to continue).

Typing is where the pairs catch people out: type じ as "ji" and ぢ as "di", ず as "zu" and づ as "du". The keyboard follows the spelling, not the sound.

Romaji traps

Hepburn romaji drops the y after j and ch, and this trips up a lot of learners:

  • じゃ, じゅ, じょ are ja, ju, jo. Not jya, jyu, jyo.
  • ちゃ, ちゅ, ちょ are cha, chu, cho. Not chya, chyu, chyo.
  • しゃ, しゅ, しょ are sha, shu, sho. Not sya, syu, syo.

After other rows the y stays: きゃ is kya, にゃ is nya, ひゃ is hya, ぎゃ is gya, びゃ is bya. The y disappears only after sh, ch and j, and the same rule applies when you type.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is writing a full-size や, ゆ or よ instead of a small one. きや is not きゃ. It is two beats, and it means something different. Write the small kana lower and smaller, and check the size before you move on.

The second mistake is forgetting the mark. か and が look alike at a glance, and the two strokes are small. Read the top right corner of every kana.

The third mistake is putting dakuten on a kana that cannot take it: only the k, s, t and h rows take dakuten, and only the h row takes handakuten. な and ま never take a mark.

The fourth mistake is treating yoon as two sounds. きょう has two beats, not three. Count beats, not kana.

How to practise

Practise in the same order the sounds build up:

  • L1 tests the basic 46. Pass it first.
  • L2 adds dakuten and handakuten, for 71.
  • L3 adds yoon, for 107.
  • L4 is the same set as L3, not a separate set.

The quiz gives the rule for moving up: "Start at L1, and move up only when you can pass the current level without looking at the chart." Do not jump to L3 while L2 still trips you up.

When you get one wrong, go back to the chart and look at that kana again: listen to the audio, watch the stroke order, and say the sound out loud. Then test yourself again. If two kana keep getting mixed up, the confusable kana guide covers the pairs that look alike.

FAQ

Do I need to learn all 107 before I can read Japanese?

No. Learn the basic 46 first, then dakuten and handakuten to reach 71, then yoon to reach 107. Most everyday words use the 46 plus dakuten; yoon shows up in words like きょう and しゃしん, so learn it soon, but not first.

Why do じ and ぢ sound the same?

The spelling is historical: modern Japanese pronounces both as ji, and both ず and づ as zu. Use じ and ず by default; ぢ and づ mainly appear inside compound words, like はなぢ and つづく.

Is the small ゃ a sound on its own?

No, it has no sound by itself. It changes the kana before it into one beat: き plus small ゃ makes きゃ, one beat, while き plus full-size や makes きや, two beats.

How do I type づ and ぢ on a keyboard?

Type "du" for づ and "di" for ぢ, "zu" for ず and "ji" for じ. The keyboard follows the spelling, so typing the sound you hear will not always give you the kana you want.

Start with L1, then add the marks

Dakuten, handakuten and yoon look small, but they are half the chart: learn the 46, add the 25 marks to reach 71, then the 36 contracted sounds to reach 107.
