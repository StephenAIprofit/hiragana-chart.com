Confusable Kana: How to Tell Similar Hiragana and Katakana Apart

Some kana look almost the same: a small loop, a hook, or the direction of a stroke is the only difference between ね and れ, or between シ and ツ. This guide works through the pairs that cause the most mistakes: nine that look alike and six spelling traps. For each pair you get what to look for, where your hand goes, and how to practise it in the quiz — the pairs come from the confusables tables on the chart.

Why these pairs cause trouble

Kana are built from a small set of strokes, so a few characters end up looking almost identical. The difference is often one loop, one hook, or the direction your hand moves. Once you know what to look for, the pairs stop being a guessing game. Work through them one at a time, and test yourself after each one.

Hiragana that look alike

ね / れ / わ (ne / re / wa)

These three share the same left side; the difference sits on the right. ね ends in a small loop. れ hooks to the right. わ curves inward and stops. The hand tells them apart: for ね, the second stroke ends in a small loop at the bottom right — close the circle. For れ, it flicks right and stops, with no loop. For わ, it sweeps down and curves in toward the centre. Try ねこ (neko, cat). Drill them at L1 Basic 46, Characters mode, Kana → Romaji, so you name each one.

さ / ち / き (sa / chi / ki)

さ and ち are mirrors: the middle stroke leans the opposite way. Count the horizontal lines to find き — it has two, while さ has one. When you write さ, draw one horizontal line across the top, then the downstroke. For き, draw two horizontals first, then the vertical and the diagonal sweep. For ち, the downstroke hooks the other way. Say さくら (sakura) as you write it. Drill them at L1, Characters mode.

ぬ / め (nu / me)

These two have the same shape, except ぬ has an extra small loop at the bottom right — the one thing to check. Write め first: two strokes ending in a loop at the bottom. For ぬ, write the same shape, then add the small loop at the bottom right. Test words: ぬの (nuno, cloth) and めがね (megane, glasses) — the extra loop means ぬ. Drill them at L1, Characters mode.

る / ろ (ru / ro)

Both start the same way: a single flowing stroke from the top. The difference is the ending: る ends in a small loop, ろ does not. So when you write る, finish by curling the stroke into a loop. For ろ, let it come back in without looping. The loop is the whole test. Try ひる (hiru, noon) and ひろい (hiroi, wide). Drill them at L1, Characters mode.

あ / お (a / o)

Both have a crossing stroke in the middle. The difference is on the right: お has a dot at the top right and a rounder right side; あ has a tighter loop. When you write あ, the right side closes into a loop. For お, touch down for the small dot at the top right, then draw the rounder right side. Try あおい (aoi, blue) and おかね (okane, money), then drill them at L1, Characters mode.

Katakana that look alike

シ / ツ (shi / tsu)

These two are made of the same three short strokes, so the difference is direction. シ is written upward and across — each stroke rises to the right. ツ is written downward and along — each stroke falls to the right. Your hand goes up for シ, down for ツ — that is the whole trick. Try シャツ (shatsu, shirt), which has both: the シ rises, the ツ falls. Switch the quiz Script to Katakana and drill them at L1, Characters mode.

ソ / ン (so / n)

ソ has two strokes and ン has one, and the direction gives them away. ソ sweeps down: write the short stroke, then a long stroke that sweeps down. ン pushes up: one stroke that ends by pushing up. Pen ending up means ン; ending down means ソ. Try ソファ (sofa) and パン (pan, bread), then drill them with Script set to Katakana, L1, Characters mode.

ク / ケ / タ (ku / ke / ta)

Count the strokes: ク has two, ケ adds a vertical on the right for three, タ crosses inside for four. Each step adds one thing: two strokes for ク, the vertical on the right for ケ, the strokes that cross inside for タ. Test words: クッキー (kukkī, cookie), ケーキ (kēki, cake), タコ (tako, octopus). Drill them with Script set to Katakana, L1, Characters mode — or Words mode, where they show up in real loanwords.

コ / ユ (ko / yu)

コ is a right angle: two strokes that meet at a clean corner. ユ extends past the corner: the vertical keeps going past the horizontal, and there is a middle horizontal line. So when you write コ, stop at the corner; for ユ, push the vertical past it. Try コーヒー (kōhī, coffee) and ユーロ (yūro, euro), then drill them with Script set to Katakana, L1, Characters mode.

Sounds and spellings that are easy to mix up

Plain vs voiced: か vs が, た vs だ. They sound close because plain Japanese sounds are not strongly aspirated, and English speakers often hear them as voiced. The mark tells you: が and だ carry a dakuten. Practise them at L2, Characters mode, with the audio on. かき (kaki, persimmon) and かぎ (kagi, key) show it in one syllable.

Same sound: じ vs ぢ, ず vs づ. じ and ぢ are the same sound in modern Japanese: ji. ず and づ are the same sound: zu. It is a typing problem: type じ as ji and ぢ as di; ず as zu and づ as du. Try じかん (jikan, time) and つづく (tsuzuku, to continue). These are L2, because both come from dakuten.

Yoon size: きや vs きゃ. きや is two beats; きゃ is one beat. The small ゃ is not a separate sound — it joins the consonant before it. Say きゃく (kyaku, guest) in one beat and you have it. That is L3, yoon. The full rules for dakuten, handakuten, and yoon are in the dakuten and yoon guide.

Hepburn spelling: じゃ / じゅ / じょ and ちゃ / ちゅ / ちょ. Hepburn drops the y in じゃ, じゅ, じょ — ja, ju, jo, not jya, jyu, jyo — and uses cha, chu, cho for ちゃ, ちゅ, ちょ, not chya, chyu, chyo. Type with the y and the quiz will not accept it. Try おちゃ (ocha, tea) and じゃがいも (jagaimo, potato). Also L3.

How to practise in the quiz

Characters mode drills the shapes; Words mode meets them in real words. Start at L1 and move up only when you can pass without looking at the chart. When you get one wrong, go back to the chart, watch the stroke order, then test yourself again. Wrong answers return later in the same session — a mistake is a second chance.

A quick checklist

  • Look at the ending: loop, hook, or inward curve.
  • Say the stroke direction out loud: up for シ, down for ツ.
  • Count the strokes: two for ク, three for ケ, four for タ.
  • A small ゃ, ゅ, or ょ is never a sound on its own.
  • Drill one pair at a time: Characters mode first, Words mode second.
