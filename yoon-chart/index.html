How yoon works

Take a kana from the i column (き, し, ち, に, ひ, み, り, and the voiced ぎ, じ, ぢ, び, ぴ) and add a small ゃ, ゅ, or ょ. The i sound disappears and the two kana become one beat: き + ゃ = きゃ (kya). The small kana has no sound of its own.

Size matters. きゃ (kya) is one beat; きや (ki-ya), with a full-size や, is two. The same rule applies in katakana: キャ is one beat, キヤ is two.

Three spellings break the romaji pattern: し row gives sha, shu, sho; ち row gives cha, chu, cho; じ row gives ja, ju, jo. The ぢ row (ぢゃ, ぢゅ, ぢょ) sounds the same as じゃ, じゅ, じょ and is rare.

For the full rules, including dakuten and handakuten, read Dakuten, Handakuten and Yoon: The Complete Guide.

Yoon questions

How many yoon are there?

36: twelve base kana (き し ち に ひ み り ぎ じ ぢ び ぴ) times three small kana (ゃ ゅ ょ). Because ぢゃ, ぢゅ, and ぢょ sound the same as じゃ, じゅ, and じょ, there are 33 different sounds. Added to the 71 kana with dakuten, they make 107.

What is the difference between きゃ and きや?

Size. In きゃ the や is small, and the pair is read as one beat: kya. In きや both kana are full size and read as two beats: ki-ya. When you write by hand, make the small kana about half the size and place it in the lower right.

Are Japanese combinations and yoon the same thing?

Yes. Yoon (拗音) is the Japanese name for these combination sounds. English learning materials often call them hiragana combinations or contracted sounds. Katakana uses the same 36, plus extra combinations for loanwords such as ファ, ティ, and ウィ, which are not part of the standard yoon set.

How do I type yoon on a keyboard?

Type the romaji: kya gives きゃ, sha gives しゃ, cha gives ちゃ. For じゃ, ja, jya, and zya all work. For the rare ぢゃ, type dya. To get a small ゃ on its own, type xya or lya.
